We have always built machines to perform as much as to work. The Mechanical Turk played chess for emperors. Chuck E. Cheese belts out songs for kids. GitHub Copilot autocompletes code for engineers. None of them are what the name suggests—and that is the point.

## Symbolic androids, not sentient heirs
These systems are symbolic androids: interfaces designed to evoke agency. They borrow credibility from theatre, not just from math. The value is the performance and the convenience, not the illusion of consciousness. Treating them like little gods is a category error.

## The theatre of intelligence
The original Mechanical Turk was a stage trick. A human operator hid inside, but the crowd was transfixed by the idea of clockwork intelligence. The lesson is not “ha, it was fake.” It is that audiences will happily suspend disbelief when the performance is useful or delightful.

## Chuck E. Cheese and the shared illusion
Every parent in the room knows the animatronic mouse is not real. Everyone plays along anyway. The shared illusion makes the interface tolerable—even charming. Modern automation relies on the same contract: ask the user to believe just enough to stay engaged.

## Copilot and math wearing a costume
Copilot is matrix algebra with a friendly name. It is astonishingly good at autocomplete, but it is still pattern matching with guardrails. When we treat it like a thinking partner, we surrender critical judgment. When we treat it like a virtuoso autocomplete, we get leverage without the reverence.

## Revolutions overlap, they don’t replace
Industrial logic powers the hardware. Information infrastructure powers the training data. Cultural stories make the illusion feel plausible. None of these revolutions erase the others; they layer. Forget that, and the robot mouse starts to feel sentient simply because it is novel.

## Practice healthy disrespect
- Enjoy the interface; interrogate the outputs.
- Ask, “What is the trick here?” before you trust.
- Trace claims to sources; flag anything unverified.
- Treat awe as a signal to slow down, not to defer.
- Design with guardrails that assume failure modes, not miracles.

These systems can amaze. They should not be worshiped. A healthy disrespect keeps us curious, skeptical, and in control—so we can build better tools and better stories.
