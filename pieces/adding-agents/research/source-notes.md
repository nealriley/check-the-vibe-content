# Adding people, adding agents

Research note, 27 October 2026.

## Starting sources

- Jan van den Berg, [“The Mythical Man-Month in the age of AI”](https://j11g.com/the-mythical-man-month-in-the-age-of-ai), 27 October 2026. A short commentary prompted by several people trying to connect a laptop to a screen. Van den Berg asks whether Brooks's staffing argument survives agents. He argues that sequential work remains sequential and that coordination shifts toward specifying success, reviewing outputs, and integrating changes. It is an argument and anecdote, with no measured comparison of human and agent teams.
- Frederick P. Brooks Jr., *The Mythical Man-Month*, originally published in 1975. The [anniversary-edition chapter record](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/mythical-man-month-the/0201835959/ch02.xhtml) identifies the book and chapter. An [original Brooks extract reproduced by Fermat's Library](https://fermatslibrary.com/s/the-mythical-man-month), first printed in *Datamation* in December 1974, discusses task division, sequential constraints, training, communication, and system testing. Those conditions matter when applying the argument to agents.

## Where to press the starting article

The suggestion that an agent becomes informed almost immediately needs examination. Starting a process, loading documents, understanding the relevant constraints, and reliably using them are different achievements. The speed of starting a session cannot establish the cost of bringing it into useful project work.

The coordination pattern also matters. Brooks's pairwise communication count describes a particular dependence between participants. A shared task board, a central coordinator, isolated assignments, and agents freely modifying one workspace create different relationships. Do not apply a quadratic overhead formula to every agent arrangement or treat it as a measured AI scaling law.

Brooks's warning concerns adding people to a late software project. Our piece can use it to examine human and agent capacity more broadly, but should state when the comparison changes the problem. Humans and agents also have different costs and responsibilities; a count of either is an incomplete description of useful capacity.

## Working comparison to investigate

| Project need | Questions when adding a person | Questions when adding an agent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learn the work | What experience and project knowledge do they bring, and how will they develop it? | What context is supplied, what can be retrieved, and how is relevant knowledge retained and checked? |
| Divide the task | Can the person work independently, and which decisions need others? | Can the assignment be bounded, and what inputs, state, and dependencies must remain consistent? |
| Coordinate changes | How are decisions, ownership, and handoffs negotiated? | How are conflicting actions, stale assumptions, and concurrent changes detected and reconciled? |
| Judge the result | Who can evaluate the work and accept responsibility for it? | What checks establish success, and who owns the resulting decision or artifact? |
| Continue after delivery | Who carries knowledge, maintenance, relationships, and commitments forward? | What persists beyond the run, and which human or organization maintains the result? |

These are editorial questions, not established performance findings. The next useful evidence is a documented project comparison with the task, coordination arrangement, elapsed time, human involvement, accepted outputs, and rework recorded. Include a case where additional agents improve the outcome as well as one where they do not.
