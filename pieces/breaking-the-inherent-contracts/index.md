# Breaking the Inherent Contracts

*Friction, Agents, and Queues*

## The idea

An essay about the implicit bargain built into many services: people can ask for help, but the effort of finding, preparing, and submitting a request limits how many do. AI can change that effort without changing the institution's duty or capacity to respond. The queue then exposes a contract that may never have been stated aloud. Who was it serving, and what should replace it?

The immediate prompt is [Ethan Mollick's post](https://x.com/emollick/status/2111595040913100837) about agentic demand for government and company services. [*Characterizing Agentic Flooding of Government Services*](research/characterizing-agentic-flooding-2608.16603v2.pdf) provides a research lead for the government side. The piece could examine the trade-offs among adding fees or limits, expanding capacity, and redesigning services so that a valid request takes less work to resolve. The company comparison needs its own evidence.

## Evidence boundary

The paper identifies 84 **potential** cases across 11 jurisdictions. Its authors say their data cannot establish that AI caused the reported surges or quantify its effect. Most cases involve people using language models to prepare text and then submitting it themselves; they do not demonstrate fully autonomous agents overwhelming services. “Inherent contracts” is this piece's interpretive frame, not the paper's term. Keep those distinctions clear when developing the argument.

## Questions to develop

- Which requests represent newly created demand, which reveal legitimate demand that was previously deterred, and which are abusive or low quality?
- Who bears the cost when an institution adds friction to control a queue?
- When can better service design absorb demand, and when does an institution need more capacity?
- What would measure access, processing time, decision quality, and fairness together?
- What evidence would show whether similar dynamics are appearing in private companies?
