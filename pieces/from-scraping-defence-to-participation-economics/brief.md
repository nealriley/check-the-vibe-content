# Working brief: From Scraping Defence to Participation Economics

## Central question

If people send agents to the web and sites increasingly turn those agents away, what terms would let machines participate legitimately?

## Existing argument and new reporting

The [writer's draft](drafts/from-scraping-defence-to-participation-economics-v2.md) begins with scraping. It treats demand for machine access as a chance to offer identified, permissioned access with clearer formats, rate limits, service levels, and optional payment. Its examples include free registration, metered API access, subscriptions for freshness, and commercial returns from adjacent sales.

The [*Verge* report](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1013195/ai-agents-blocked-web) widens the question from a crawler taking content to a personal agent trying to act for someone. It reports access restrictions or failures at commerce, travel, review, and financial sites, and notes that some failures arise from general anti-bot systems. Site owners have reasons to protect accounts, inventory, data, and their own customer relationships; users also have a legitimate interest in choosing software to represent them. That conflict is the new opening for the essay.

## Possible shape

1. Begin with a person whose agent cannot complete a useful task because the destination site does not admit it. Show whether the barrier is an explicit policy, an anti-bot check, or an uncertain failure.
2. Step back to the draft's scraping question: blocking is one response to demand for machine access, but the site can also specify what a known machine may receive.
3. Move from access to **participation**: identity, representation, delegated authority, permitted actions, rate limits, data formats, revocation, and accountability. Separate reading public material from entering an account or placing an order.
4. Explore possible economic arrangements: free authenticated access, paid delivery, subscriptions, and transactions supported by the resulting relationship. Ask who pays and what value they receive.
5. Test the optimistic thesis. A site might have good reasons to refuse outside agents; payment can exclude smaller participants; identity systems can concentrate control. Participation is a negotiated choice, not the inevitable destination for every blocked bot.

## Questions to resolve

- How does a site verify that an agent represents a particular user and that the user approved a particular action?
- Which terms can be standardized, and which depend on a site's risk, economics, and customer relationship?
- Does a machine-readable feed create a new service, or merely shift traffic away from a site's human interface?
- Where do content licensing, user-authorized action, and payment intersect, and where must they remain separate?
- What evidence would show that a participation model works for smaller publishers as well as large platforms?
