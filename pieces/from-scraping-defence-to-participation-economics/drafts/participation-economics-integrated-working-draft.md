# From Scraping Defence to Participation Economics

Working adaptation of the writer's [V2 draft](from-scraping-defence-to-participation-economics-v2.md), incorporating the [17 October 2026 *Verge* report](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1013195/ai-agents-blocked-web). This is an exploratory draft, not a fact-checked final essay.

There is a lot of chatter about AI systems scraping the web and what content owners ought to do about it. Cloudflare's AI Crawl Control has made the choice visible as a platform feature: a site owner can watch, allow, or block AI crawlers, with pay-per-crawl access in closed beta. Most of the conversation focuses on prevention. How do we stop the bots?

Now another kind of machine visitor is running into the same closed doors. In [The Verge's account](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1013195/ai-agents-blocked-web), people ask personal agents to shop, book, or manage services for them, only to discover that the destination site will not let the agent through. Some sites have decided against outside agents; elsewhere, ordinary anti-bot systems appear to catch agents that a business might otherwise welcome. A person can authorize an assistant to act for them, but that does not automatically tell the site who the assistant is, what it may do, or why the site should trust it.

A crawler collecting pages and an agent trying to buy a ticket are not the same visitor. One asks for content; the other may need account access and permission to change something. But both expose a missing relationship. The web has ways to serve a page to a browser and ways to fend off unwanted automation. It has fewer widely accepted ways for an outside machine to introduce itself, establish its authority, and receive a useful level of service on stated terms.

The very fact that content is being scraped tells you something: there is demand for it. Someone thinks the information is worth acquiring and storing. That does not make every scraper a potential paying customer. Demand is not the same as willingness to buy. But it does suggest that blocking and licensing are only two possible responses to a machine audience.

Humans render pages. Machines often have little use for the presentation layer. An agent may retrieve the underlying HTML, but modern pages can make a simple thought surprisingly hard to extract. Clean text, structured data, or a direct interface could be a better service than asking every machine to reverse engineer the human page. Once that service exists, a site can decide who receives it and under which conditions.

One participation model does not involve payment at all. A machine registers an identity and receives a defined level of access. Like an account for a person, that identity can come with entitlements: how many requests it may make, how quickly the site responds, which data it can see, and whether the answer arrives as a page, Markdown, a file, or an API response. This is a more legible exchange than anonymous traffic followed by a block, though identity alone says little about whether the machine is acting for a particular user.

Other arrangements may involve money. A site could charge per request when delivery has a clear marginal cost, offer a subscription for fresh and reliable data, or keep access free because the relationship supports an adjacent sale. OpenAlex, Crossref, and Amazon's Creators API illustrate different ways to separate the underlying information from the terms of delivery or the commercial return. None proves that every publisher can turn scraping into a business, or that every agent will accept the terms.

The personal-agent case adds a harder question. A paid content feed does not authorize an agent to buy on someone's behalf, read their account, or move their money. Those actions need a way to bind the agent to a person, specify what the person approved, limit what can be done, and let both sides revoke access. The service needs to know what it has agreed to do; the person needs to know what they have delegated. A partnership can solve this for one provider and one site. A broader web of agents would need arrangements that work beyond a handful of bilateral deals.

Some sites will still say no. They may be protecting customers from fraud, preventing inventory abuse, or defending a business that depends on the human interface. Those concerns do not disappear because a user wants to send an agent. At the same time, an indiscriminate block can prevent the customer from using a tool they chose. The useful question is less whether every agent should be admitted than what a site would need to know before it could admit one safely and on fair terms.

That is the move from scraping defence to participation economics. The site defines access; the machine presents an identity and a purpose; the person grants authority where an action concerns them; and the terms may be free, paid, or connected to another exchange. The promise is not that all scraping becomes revenue or that all agents get through. It is that a relationship made explicit can offer more than a contest between disguised automation and the next closed door.
