# Working brief: How to Talk About AI

## Role in the series

An opening essay that gives readers a way to talk about AI without treating every model, product, agent, and ambition as the same thing. This is a thematic introduction, not a compressed version of every later piece.

The writer's [recording transcript](research/recording-transcript.md) offers a concrete route into that problem: AI can read and draft email or answer a phone call, yet often leaves the inherited communication system in place. The inbox is an example readers know, not the whole subject of the essay.

## Reader question

What changes when systems can make increasingly capable decisions, and why do we keep describing and using them through the structures we already know?

## Working proposition

Technological revolutions overlap. Computing and networks changed industrial production; AI now builds on computing and data while also changing what those systems can do. But capability does not by itself tell us what to build. Much current AI work makes an old process faster or imitates a person inside it. A better conversation begins by naming the parts of the system, asking what human purpose the process serves, and considering which structures are still useful.

Email makes the distinction tangible. Open mail protocols let people and software communicate across networks. The inbox is one way to present and manage that communication. AI that searches the inbox or drafts replies may help, but it does not settle how people should manage relationships, commitments, requests, and shared context. The essay can use this example to invite sharper questions rather than prescribe a replacement interface.

## Possible movement of the essay

1. **Begin in a familiar place.** An assistant clears messages or answers calls, but the work still arrives through an inbox or phone tree. Ask what has changed and what has not.
2. **Widen the lens.** Move from tools to automation, conditional decisions, and today's model-driven systems. Present the revolutions as overlapping forces, not a neat historical sequence.
3. **Find the vocabulary.** Distinguish a model from the product around it, imitation from representation, and task completion from a useful human outcome. Explain why saying only “AI” hides these differences.
4. **Return to communication.** Separate email's interoperable protocols from the inbox metaphor. Ask whether messages should remain the main unit of attention when the underlying work may be a relationship, agreement, request, or community.
5. **Open the series.** Leave readers with better questions about history, intelligence, learning, outputs, agents, and human responsibility. The later pieces can answer them in detail.

## Boundaries and open questions

- Do not present the movement from tools to AI as inevitable progress or imply that all automation is AI.
- Do not treat passing or approaching a Turing test as a measure of general intelligence; the recording uses it as a provocative illustration of imitation.
- Keep the distinction between *email as a set of protocols* and *the inbox as an interface*. Replacing one does not require replacing the other.
- “My agent talks to your agent” is a question about representation, authority, shared context, and trust. This essay need not solve its technical design.
- The recording's strongest communication argument could support a separate piece later. Here it should make the series' broader questions concrete.

## Claims to check before drafting

- The history of inbox, desk-tray, and carbon-copy metaphors, and how closely those metaphors actually shaped email clients.
- Which current AI products answer calls, manage email, or mediate person-to-person communication, and whether they primarily imitate existing workflows.
- The cost and practical barriers of global digital communication; “essentially free” is a useful contrast but too absolute as a factual claim.
- The state of agent-to-agent communication and relationship-centered tools. Avoid saying that nobody works on these problems without a current survey.
- What the Turing test can and cannot establish, and the history of the particular tests being invoked.
