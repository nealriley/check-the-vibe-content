# How to Talk About AI

*Transcript-based working draft. Examples and technical claims need checking before publication.*

Every new technological revolution seems to arrive before we have the words to describe it. We are living through what I think of as an intelligence revolution, but we have limited shared context for what this technology is, how it works, and how it meets people in everyday life. That is more than an inconvenience. Without a useful vocabulary, we misrepresent its capabilities, miss its benefits, and fail to recognize the harm it can do in a particular setting.

Consider how often one name stands in for a whole landscape. In conversation, *ChatGPT* can become a catch-all for models, applications, assistants, and other systems with very different jobs. It is a little like calling every cotton bud a Q-tip. The familiar name makes the subject easy to point at, but it makes the differences harder to see. If we cannot say which part of a system we mean, how can we judge what it can do, what it costs, or whether it has worked?

The word *agent* has a similar problem. When I ask an agent to do something for me, I mean that it acts on my behalf. But when people call a system *agentic*, they may be talking about a different property: its ability to choose steps, use tools, and continue working without being told every move. Those two meanings touch, but they are not identical. A system can have room to make decisions while the person who set it to work still carries responsibility for what it does. The word alone does not tell us how much authority the system has, where its work happens, or when it must stop and ask.

Think about the difference between a chat service that can call a few tools and a coding agent running in a workspace with access to files and commands. Both might be called agents. Their useful range and their risks depend on their environment, permissions, and the work they have been given. “My agent did it” is not enough of an explanation. Which agent? Acting where? With whose authority? What could it see and change?

Even *model* is beginning to lose its edges. We speak of a model as though all models produce the same kind of answer. Some generate text. Others work with images, audio, or video. Some are used to classify or estimate; others are built to learn representations or support decisions in a larger system. Their training, outputs, and suitable measures can differ. A model can also be only one part of a product: the interface, stored context, retrieval, tools, rules, and human choices around it all shape what a user experiences. Asking whether “the AI” is accurate or safe may conceal the layer where a failure actually occurred.

The language around connections is also becoming compressed. People say they have “connected an MCP” as though a protocol were a single tool or a complete integration strategy. Standards and reusable instructions can help systems work with other software, but there is more than one way to connect a system to the world. The mechanism matters. So do the permissions and limits of the environment in which it runs. We need words that let us discuss those choices rather than treating connection as a magical property of a model.

Then there is the hardest word of all: *intelligence*. Before this wave of artificial intelligence, many of us might have thought we knew what we meant by it. But the more closely we look, the less useful a single human-shaped definition becomes. Different animals sense, learn, predict, and act in different ways. Artificial systems can perform activities we readily call intelligent, especially when they use language we understand. That does not mean their intelligence works like ours, or that fluent speech proves a human-like mind behind it.

I do not think we need to settle one final definition before we can talk. We do need to say which ability we are discussing: prediction, learning, reasoning, adaptation, planning, generation, or something else. We need to distinguish the model from the service, a service from an agent, and an agent's freedom to act from the human authority it is supposed to represent. These words are approximations, but more careful approximations would let us disagree about the same thing.

That is where I want to begin. Not with a verdict on whether AI is good or bad, intelligent or unintelligent, inevitable or overhyped. I want a language clear enough to ask what a particular system does, how it does it, what we expect from it, and who is answerable when it enters our lives.
