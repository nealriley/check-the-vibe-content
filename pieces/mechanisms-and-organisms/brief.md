# Working brief

## What the writer asked for

- Start from Alan Watts's talk to IBM on mechanisms and organisms. A good YouTube recording exists, and a transcript can be made from it.
- Tell a 2026 version of the same story, using the technology available now.
- Cover the ground Watts would cover, without saying explicitly that this is what Watts would say. Name him as the inspiration and call back to the talk.
- Structure: what technology was like then, what conversation was being had, then bring that conversation forward to today.

## To confirm

- The date and setting. The video title says IBM, 1969; one account also puts IBM hiring Watts in 1969, while a 2007 blog post guessed the early 1970s. Look for an independent record.
- Quotations against the audio. The transcript is cleaned from automatic captions and marks its guesses.

## Questions to develop

- What did IBM's machines do in the late 1960s, and how did the people building them describe them?
- Where does the mechanism/organism distinction hold up against today's models and agents, which are trained or grown rather than assembled part by part?
- Where does it break down? Today's systems are still built and run as mechanisms, even if their behaviour isn't designed piece by piece.
- What would the equivalent audience be in 2026: AI lab engineers, platform builders, or everyone now using agents?

## What the talk covers

The talk's own title on the upload is "Digital Technology – The Process of Control". In likely spoken order:

1. **The problem of control.** Who guards the guards? Rational control of nature, outside and now inside the skin, breeds a complexity in which "we're standing in our own way". Paperwork crowds out business, medicine and teaching.
2. **Triangulation.** Watts studies other cultures to expose the hidden assumptions of one's own. Then comes human ecology, and the question of what you would do if you were God.
3. **The dream thought experiment.** With total control, you would eventually choose to forget you were in control. Are we wise enough to play at being God?
4. **Made versus grown.** The West sees the universe as a construct ("How was I made?"). We have "bitted the cosmos": the fisherman's net, the grid, measurement, calculus.
5. **Prickles and goo.** These are two temperaments. Nature is "gooey prickles and prickly goo", depending on the level of magnification.
6. **Spotlight versus floodlight.** Scanning attention notices only what it has notation for. A thing is a "think", a unit of thought.
7. **Mechanism versus organism.** A mechanism is assembled; an organism grows. The gallbladder specialist is the example.
8. **Information explosion.** Nobody can read every catalogue. Parkinson's Law means computers keep you ahead "only for a short time".
9. **Pattern recognition.** Machines struggle to read a handwritten A and lack "field organisation". Are we leaving out our strongest suit?
10. **Self-reference.** The teeth can't bite themselves, which he compares to Gödel. Total self-transparency would leave no surprise.
11. **Insight, then checking.** The brain hands you the answer, and bit-by-bit analysis checks it, often too late, because life comes at you all at once.
12. **Right foot, left foot.** This is not anti-technology: alternate the analytic with respect for organic organisation.
13. **Trust versus control.** Distrusting our nature, we project control into clocks, police and institutions. We confuse the symbol with the reality: the menu with the dinner, the money with the wealth.

## Then and now: first notes

Several of the 1969 claims now point the other way, and that tension could carry the piece:

- **Pattern recognition.** In 1969 machines failed to read a handwritten A. Today's models are themselves pattern recognisers, closer to the floodlight than the spotlight.
- **Grown, not assembled.** Neural networks are trained rather than programmed line by line, and their builders cannot fully explain the result. That is closer to Watts's organism than his mechanism, yet they still run on bitted hardware and are sold as products.
- **Information explosion.** "Who has read all the catalogues?" now has an answer: a model can read them. Parkinson's Law still applies to the volume of generated output.
- **Insight versus checking.** Models now produce the fast, unexplained answer, and humans do the slow verification. The roles may have swapped.
- **Who guards the guards.** AI alignment, oversight and evaluation are the 2026 form of the control problem.
- **Symbol and reality.** Benchmarks, metrics and generated text risk becoming the menu that gets mistaken for the dinner.
