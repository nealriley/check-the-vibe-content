# Dialogue plan: the key ideas

A self-contained plan for the conversation between the writer and Claude about Alan Watts's talk [Seeing Through the Net](../source-material/seeing-through-the-net-organism-earth.md) (IBM, 1969). It was written on 1 November 2026 so that the conversation can restart in a new session. The full history of the first attempt is in [the dialogue log](dialogue-log.md). The beat numbers refer to [the talk map](../talk-map.md).

The purpose is to bring out new thoughts from the writer that can drive the piece's narrative. When the dialogue is finished, its results go into a new brief.

## How to run it

- **Claude leads.** Take one idea at a time. Give Watts's point briefly, then Claude's own thinking about where it lands today, then a thread the writer can pull or ignore. Don't ask the writer to choose topics or recall their favourite lines.
- **It is a dialogue, not an interview.** Respond to what the writer says with ideas: agree, extend, connect to another of Watts's points, or push back. Don't ask for examples or drill into one case. If the writer gives an example, take its point and move on.
- **Keep it moving.** One or two exchanges per idea is enough unless the writer wants more. Then go on to the next idea.
- **Watch the language.** Claude's habit is to fall back on mechanical framing: bottlenecks, pipelines, queues, flow, "who owns this one decision". The writer corrected this twice in the first attempt. Talk about relationships, growth and knock-on effects across the whole organisation instead.
- **Log as you go.** Record the writer's words closely in [the dialogue log](dialogue-log.md), summarise Claude's responses, and note what's settled and what's open under each idea.
- **By voice:** the writer may answer through TalkBox. Spoken messages should be one or two plain sentences.

## What the first attempt settled

The writer's lens is modern times: how businesses run, how technology has come along, and specifically how AI has been introduced into work. Points already established:

- **AI is a facsimile** of human actions, thoughts, intentions and intuitions. It continues the line of the copier and the mainframe that replaced the human "computer", and is not a clean break.
- **What sets it apart** is breadth (trained on everything) and function: judgement calls and digital action. At its simplest, those are "what should I write next?" and "is this done?". The line runs from replacing labour, to replacing a role, to replacing judgement.
- **The central criticism:** the AI debate (including AI tools themselves) assesses AI labour in isolation, asking either "can it replace the role?" or "is this task's output good enough?". Watts's alternative is that an organisation is closer to an organism than a mechanism, so replacing even part of one role has knock-on effects throughout.
- **The hard part is measurement.** The effects spread across the whole, and most organisations don't measure themselves that way.
- **The writer's worked example:** a development team used AI to clear a backlog.
  - Engineers for whom review wasn't the main job were drawn into reviewing. Others sped up and drifted away from the day-to-day.
  - Once knowing what to build became the scarce thing, a forward deployed engineer role emerged, and the business began favouring taste-maker customers over niche ones.
  - The team is flat, so it spotted the need quickly.
- **Mechanism versus organism** has become central through the writer's own answers, despite the earlier instruction that no theme should lead. Confirm before writing the brief.

## The ideas

### 1. Who guards the guards

**Watts:** the whole talk is framed as the problem of control. Every control needs a controller, and control piles up until "we're standing in our own way". The business drowns in hassles, the hospital in records and the university in the registrar. (beats 1–3)

**Where it lands now:** AI makes guarding almost free. Models check models, agents review agents' work, and AI summarises the audit. When guarding costs almost nothing, the guards can multiply without limit, and perhaps the question becomes whether anyone still remembers what was being guarded. Paperwork also returns: the writer noted how much paper is still used nearly sixty years on.

**Thread:** if guarding is free, is restraint the scarce thing now?

### 2. What would you do if you were God?

**Watts:** we are gaining the power to shape our environment, and even ourselves, beyond anyone before us. So what do we want? Write twenty pages on your idea of heaven and you find you don't want it. In the dream thought experiment, total control leads to boredom, and in the end you choose to forget you are in control so that you can be surprised. (beats 5–9)

**Where it lands now:** AI puts a business close to that position for output. Code, copy and images are available on request. Friction used to be how people found out what they wanted, and without it, "what do you want?" becomes most of the job, which matches the writer's point that clarity is now scarce. Perhaps the dream's ending is already visible too, in "vibe coding" and in accepting whatever comes back: people choosing to give up control in order to be surprised.

**Thread:** is letting the machine surprise you wisdom, as Watts's dream suggests, or abdication?

### 3. Made or grown

**Watts:** the West asks "How was I made?" rather than "How was I grown?". A mechanism is assembled from parts. An organism grows, and the gallbladder specialist cuts out one part without foreseeing the effects elsewhere, because a body is not a car with replaceable spark plugs. (beats 10, 16)

**Where it lands now:** the models are grown (trained, not programmed), yet they are bought, versioned and governed as if they were made. Organisations are the same: the org chart is drawn as if made, while culture grows. AI roll-out plans are drawn as part replacements, and their effects arrive as growth, which is the writer's central point.

**Thread:** what would it mean to grow an AI adoption rather than install one?

### 4. The net

**Watts:** we understand the world by cutting it into bits, "bits of information". We throw a net over a wiggly world, and its grid isn't in the water. A thing is a "think", a unit of thought. Spotlight attention notices only what we have notation for. (beats 11–12, 14–15)

**Where it lands now:** a language model is made entirely of the net: our words for the world, recombined. When a business feeds it its documents, it gets the business's net and nothing else. Anything that was never written down, such as tacit knowledge, relationships and the mood of a room, falls through the mesh. The writer's phrase "the environment as it's represented around it" is the same point.

**Thread:** what in an organisation never makes it into the net, and what happens to it when more and more work runs through the net alone?

### 5. Prickles and goo

**Watts:** there are two temperaments: the prickly (rigour, statistics, precision) and the gooey (vagueness, intuition, mysticism). Nature is "gooey prickles and prickly goo", depending on how closely you look. (beat 13)

**Where it lands now:** AI is oddly both: statistics underneath, fluent and vague on top, a prickly machine that speaks goo. The business argument about AI splits the same way: evaluation, metrics and benchmarks on one side, and taste, judgement and "vibes" on the other.

**Thread:** is the current talk of "taste" the goo returning to an organisation that had become all prickles?

### 6. The information explosion

**Watts:** who has read all the catalogues? Computers keep you ahead "only for a short time", because work expands to fill what is available. The drummers out-complicate each other until they need a gadget to keep count. (beats 17–18)

**Where it lands now:** AI can read all the catalogues, and it writes them too. Machines summarise text that machines wrote. It is the drummers' contest at machine speed, and the gadget that keeps count has become another drummer.

**Thread:** does anything end the contest, or is that the wrong hope, as Watts would say, because the contest is what the organism does?

### 7. Our strongest suit

**Watts:** a computer can't read a handwritten "a". It lacks "field organisation" and sees in dots, like a newspaper photograph. Pattern recognition is the brain's strongest suit. Education rewards verbal, mnemonic and computational intelligence and neglects the bodily and social kinds. (beats 19, 25)

**Where it lands now:** he was wrong about the limit, and that's useful. The floodlight was supposed to be ours. If the machine can recognise patterns, either we have lost our strongest suit, or it was never pattern recognition: perhaps it is being in the world, having a body, or caring how things turn out. The three intelligences that education rewarded are now exactly what AI does cheaply.

**Thread:** once those three are cheap, what will organisations begin to reward?

### 8. The teeth can't bite themselves

**Watts:** we can't model our own brains, and he compares this to Gödel. If we became completely transparent to ourselves there would be no surprise left: we'd be God again, and bored. (beats 20–21)

**Where it lands now:** labs use AI to try to understand AI. Businesses use AI to watch themselves: dashboards and summaries of the organisation, written for the organisation. It is an organisation trying to see itself completely through its own instruments.

**Thread:** what would a company lose if it became perfectly legible to itself?

### 9. Answers first, checking after

**Watts:** the best discoveries come without thinking. The brain hands you the answer, and the step-by-step checking comes afterwards, because only that counts socially. Life comes "from all sides, all at once", so careful knowledge often arrives too late. (beats 22–23)

**Where it lands now:** the roles have swapped. The machine supplies the fast, unexplained answer, and the person does the slow checking. In effect, we have handed intuition to the machine and kept the slow part for ourselves. The writer's example showed engineers drawn into reviewing work outside their core role.

**Thread:** what happens to a person's own intuition when most of their job is checking someone else's?

### 10. Right foot, left foot

**Watts:** this isn't anti-technology. The technological enterprise put its right foot forward, and now it must bring up the left: "a new respect for the organic kind of organisation". Technology that relies only on linear thinking risks becoming the dinosaur with two brains. (beats 24, 26)

**Where it lands now:** most AI roll-outs are all right foot: speed, output and headcount. The left foot isn't slowing down. It is a different kind of attention, to the organisation as something living.

**Thread:** what does a left step look like in a company adopting AI, and who takes it?

### 11. Trust, and the menu and the dinner

**Watts:** if we think human nature can't be trusted, we build control systems (church, police, clocks) and they are really ourselves, disguised. We change the clocks instead of getting up earlier. We confuse the symbol with the reality: the money with the wealth, the menu with the dinner, until we are "starving on eating menus". (beats 27–29)

**Where it lands now:** AI adoption is measured in menus: seats licensed, tokens used, tasks completed. Guardrails and approval steps may say more about distrust of the people in the organisation than of the machine. Moving the clocks has a modern form: changing the measure instead of the behaviour.

**Thread:** what would introducing AI on the basis of trust look like, and is that naive or the only thing that works?

## After the ideas: format, Watts's place, title

Debate these once the ideas are done. Claude argues the case for and against each shape after the writer reacts, not before.

- **Then, then now.** 1969, the conversation Watts had, then the same conversation retold in 2026. This is the original brief, and it is clear and faithful. The risk is that it reads as a book report.
- **The overturned claim.** Open with the handwritten "a", the claim that failed, then ask which of his claims failed and which turned out truer than he knew. It is argumentative and strong as an essay, but it may put the technology ahead of the organisation.
- **The same room, twice.** An IBM seminar room in 1969 and a 2026 room, alternating. This is the most narrative shape, and it risks becoming "what Watts would say", which the brief rules out.
- **The dialogue.** A writer and an AI work through the talk, and the AI's slips into mechanical framing become evidence for the argument. This emerged from the first attempt. It is vivid, but it may make the piece about AI rather than about organisations.
- **The lecture, rewritten.** A 2026 talk to a modern audience, crediting Watts as the inspiration. It is closest to his voice, and furthest from the brief.

Also decide:
- Where Watts sits in the piece: guide, foil, ghost or speaker.
- The title. "Mechanisms and Organisms" may or may not still fit.
