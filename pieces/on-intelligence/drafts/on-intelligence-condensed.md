# Machine Intelligence Is Intelligence Grown Under Glass

Artificial intelligence is becoming too capable, too common, and too strange for our usual definitions to remain untouched.

People now encounter machines that answer questions, write code, summarize science, draft emails, explain jokes, generate images, and argue about whether they are intelligent. That encounter unsettles us because it touches one of our most human-defining words.

The public conversation has split into two thin stories. One treats these systems as new minds arriving in software. The other dismisses them as autocomplete with better marketing. Both stories contain something true. These systems are not human minds, and they are built from statistical machinery. But neither fact explains the behavior we are seeing.

The better question is not whether machines possess some mysterious essence called intelligence. It is how intelligence is made. What processes shape a system until it can use information, respond to pressure, learn from correction, model possibilities, and act effectively?

This is why I prefer machine intelligence to artificial intelligence. Artificial implies fake or counterfeit. Machine names the substrate and the process. The issue is not whether intelligence is unreal because it is made. The issue is what kind of intelligence a machine-made process can produce.

Here is the working definition: intelligence is the capacity of a system to use information adaptively, selecting actions that improve its fit to an environment, task, or goal.

Information includes more than facts. It includes sensory signals, inherited structure, memories, language, images, rewards, errors, and probabilities. Action includes more than movement. Prediction, classification, speech, planning, generation, tool use, and internal simulation are actions in this sense. Fit means effectiveness under pressure: survival, reward, loss reduction, benchmark performance, user approval, social success, or task completion.

This definition does not require consciousness, human likeness, biological life, IQ, selfhood, or emotion. Those may matter, but they are not the entry fee. Intelligence is adaptive fit-making.

Once we define intelligence this way, it becomes harder to treat it as one thing. A baby, raven, dog, chimpanzee, octopus, nematode, mathematician, and language model do not possess the same capacity in different quantities. They express different forms of adaptive competence in different worlds.

Organisms do not begin as blank receivers of data. Evolution has already shaped their bodies, senses, reflexes, drives, and nervous systems. Inheritance comes first. Sensing and action then arise together: creatures move, eat, avoid, reach, explore, test, and fail. Feedback gives the process direction. Memory preserves useful changes. Learning tunes behavior. Modeling lets organisms anticipate patterns. Simulation lets richer organisms test possibilities internally. Social learning, language, and tools make intelligence cumulative.

Daniel Dennett gives a useful ladder for these layers. Darwinian creatures are shaped by selection across generations. Skinnerian creatures learn from reward, punishment, and trial-and-error within a lifetime. Popperian creatures test possibilities internally before risking action in the world. Gregorian creatures inherit mind-tools from culture: language, maps, numbers, writing, diagrams, institutions, and science.

Human beings are all of these at once. We are evolved bodies. We learn through feedback. We simulate futures before acting. We inherit cultural tools we did not invent. Our intelligence is not sealed inside the skull; it is scaffolded by language, tools, other people, and external records.

Language matters because it does not merely express thought. It changes what thought can do. Words make categories portable. Writing makes memory external. Diagrams make relations inspectable. Equations make abstractions manipulable. Books let one mind work with the products of many others. Human intelligence is already extended through mind-tools.

That matters for machines because large language models are not trained on raw nature. They are trained on the residue of human mind-tools: explanations, procedures, arguments, code, laws, recipes, stories, documentation, jokes, and scientific records. To call this "just text" is to misunderstand text. Text is one of humanity's main vehicles for externalized thought.

Still, language is not the same as understanding. Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson argue that verbal understanding is not just encoding and decoding. It is inferential: we infer meaning from context, intention, relevance, shared background, tone, and social situation. LLMs can be fluent because they inherit immense patterns of language, but fluency is not the whole of understanding. Understanding has layers: linguistic competence, pragmatic inference, grounded experience, social accountability, practical consequence, and internal simulation. Machines can be strong in some layers and weak in others.

That unevenness is the key to machine intelligence.

Silicon is not inherently intelligent, but carbon is not inherently intelligent either. Intelligence appears when a substrate is organized by processes that create adaptive fit. For machines, those processes include architecture, data, training, prediction, error correction, reward, selection, simulation, memory-like weights, and deployment feedback.

The biological elements have machine corollaries. Inheritance becomes architecture, pretrained weights, datasets, prior model generations, and engineering assumptions. Input becomes text, images, audio, video, game states, prompts, robot sensors, API results, and benchmark tasks. Feedback becomes loss functions, reward models, human ratings, failures, benchmark scores, and user corrections. Memory becomes weights, context windows, retrieval systems, fine-tuning, logs, and stored representations. Action becomes prediction, generation, classification, tool calls, code execution, game moves, robotic movement, and planning.

Machine intelligence is not born. It is trained, selected, corrected, and deployed.

The Darwinian analogue is selection. Biological evolution is wild, open-ended, embodied, and multi-pressure. Machine selection is narrower, faster, more intentional, and more instrumented. Researchers choose architectures that perform better. Benchmarks reward some capabilities and neglect others. Training methods survive if they reduce loss or fit compute budgets. Products survive if users and markets keep them alive. This is not literal natural selection, but it is selection among variants under pressure.

The Skinnerian analogue is feedback. AlphaGo and AlphaZero learn in constrained worlds where success is measurable: win the game. Reinforcement learning, reward functions, self-play, RLHF, and preference optimization all shape behavior through consequences. RLHF does not give a model morality or lived social understanding, but it does act as behavioral correction: some outputs are rewarded, others are discouraged.

The Popperian analogue is simulation. Popperian intelligence lets hypotheses die before the organism does. Machine versions include search, planning systems, model-based reinforcement learning, world models, tree search, simulators, and JEPA-style predictive models. LLMs complicate this layer. They may have a linguistic model of the world, but not an embodied, action-grounded one. They do not model the world the way animals do. They model the human record of world-modeling.

Their greatest strength is Gregorian. LLMs are trained on the accumulated symbolic output of human civilization: language, code, science, stories, essays, arguments, laws, tutorials, and explanations. They are not trained on the world directly. They are trained on the world as processed by humans. They inherit our categories, procedures, analogies, discoveries, biases, and confusions. They are trained on the fossil record of human cognition.

Machine Gregorian intelligence is derivative in one sense. It rests on human cultural capital. But human Gregorian intelligence is also derivative. Every educated human inherits words, maps, concepts, norms, methods, and institutions they did not invent. The difference is that humans inherit them as embodied organisms living in the world. LLMs inherit them as machine systems trained on symbolic residue.

Not all AI is the same. LLMs showcase language, cultural inheritance, synthesis, and tool-mediated work. AlphaGo showcases reinforcement, search, and strategic discovery. RLHF showcases preference shaping. JEPA and world-model research showcase predictive abstraction. Robotics showcases perception, action, embodiment, friction, and physical feedback. Tool-using agents showcase action in symbolic environments.

No single system mirrors the whole organismic stack. Machine intelligence is plural because organismic intelligence is plural.

That plurality also explains failure. One failure mode is Ralph Wiggum mode: fluent language with the shape of understanding that collapses into absurdity, literalness, misplaced confidence, or context error. It happens when a system inherits the sentence pattern without enough of the world-structure behind it. Gregorian intelligence outruns Popperian and embodied intelligence.

This is not a reason to deny machine intelligence. It is a reason to describe it accurately. Machine systems can have real adaptive capacities: prediction, pattern recognition, abstraction, linguistic fluency, synthesis, tool use, planning-like behavior, code generation, strategic search, and responsiveness to feedback. But they generally lack organismic need, metabolism, mortality, childhood, biological embodiment, intrinsic survival pressure, direct lived experience, durable personal identity, and full sensorimotor grounding.

An animal learns because the world can hurt, feed, threaten, reward, or kill it. A model learns because a training process adjusts parameters in response to error, reward, or preference. Both are feedback, but not the same kind of feedback.

This is what I mean by intelligence grown under glass. Biological intelligence is evolved in the wild, shaped by messy, overlapping, open-ended pressures. Machine intelligence is evolved in the lab, shaped by targeted pressures chosen by researchers, companies, benchmarks, users, and markets. The process is artificial, but artificial does not mean unreal.

A lab-grown crystal is still a crystal. Cultured tissue is still tissue. A domesticated animal is still an evolved animal. But production history matters. Lab-grown intelligence carries the marks of the laboratory: brittleness outside distribution, benchmark-shaped competence, dependence on human data, optimization for approval, lack of embodied stakes, sudden gaps, and surprising strengths.

Machine intelligence is not a god, not a person, not a conscious animal, and not merely autocomplete. It is a lab-grown form of adaptive competence produced by machine processes acting on human cultural inheritance.

The non-magical explanation is astonishing enough. We have built systems that run training, feedback, selection, simulation, and optimization over the externalized products of human thought. They do not need to be human to matter. They do not need to be conscious to exhibit intelligence. They need to be understood as the kind of thing they are: machine intelligence, grown under glass.
