# Machine Intelligence Is Intelligence Grown Under Glass

## The Vocabulary Problem

Artificial intelligence is becoming too capable, too common, and too strange for our usual definitions to survive unchanged.

People now encounter machines that can answer questions, write code, summarize science, translate between languages, draft legal clauses, imitate sympathy, explain jokes, generate images, reason through puzzles, and argue about whether they are intelligent. These systems are no longer hidden in laboratories. They are in search boxes, writing tools, classrooms, offices, phones, code editors, customer-service windows, and private conversations.

That encounter is unsettling because it touches one of our most human-defining words: intelligence.

The public conversation has split into two poor stories. In one story, these systems are new minds arriving in software, alien intelligences or proto-gods whose powers we can barely comprehend. In the other story, they are merely autocomplete with better marketing, statistical tricks that imitate thought without approaching it. Both stories contain something true. These systems are not human minds. They are built from statistical machinery. But neither fact explains the full range of behavior we are seeing.

The problem is not that everyone has picked the wrong side. The problem is that our vocabulary is too thin. We use one word, intelligence, for Stephen Hawking, ordinary adults, babies, ravens, dogs, chimps, octopuses, nematodes, AlphaGo, ChatGPT, and robot vacuums. Then we act surprised when the category strains.

The better question is not whether machines possess some magical essence called intelligence. It is how intelligence is made. What processes shape a system until it can use information, respond to pressure, learn from correction, model possibilities, and act effectively? If we can answer that question for organisms, we can begin to ask what version of that process is happening in machines.

This is why I prefer the phrase machine intelligence to artificial intelligence.

## From Artificial Intelligence To Machine Intelligence

Artificial intelligence carries a confusing implication. Artificial often means fake, counterfeit, imitation, or lesser-than. An artificial flower is not a flower. Artificial flavor is not the thing itself. So when we say artificial intelligence, we risk smuggling in the answer before asking the question. We imply that the intelligence is unreal because it is made.

Machine intelligence is a cleaner phrase. It names the substrate and the production method. These systems are made of machines, trained by machine processes, evaluated in machine-readable environments, and increasingly connected to machine tools. The phrase does not decide in advance whether the resulting capacity is real or fake. It asks us to look at the way the capacity is produced.

By machine intelligence, I mean intelligence-like behavior produced through machine processes: training, prediction, feedback, selection, optimization, simulation, architecture, data inheritance, and deployment. It is not a ghost in silicon. It is a stack of operations that, when organized in the right way, can exhibit adaptive competence.

The central claim of this essay is simple: machine intelligence is not human intelligence, but it is not nothing. It is lab-grown intelligence, a form of adaptive competence produced by machine processes acting on inherited human cultural material inside controlled digital environments.

To make that claim useful, we need a working definition of intelligence.

## Intelligence As Adaptive Fit-Making

Intelligence is the capacity of a system to use information adaptively, selecting actions that improve its fit to an environment, task, or goal.

Information does not only mean facts. It includes sensory signals, inherited structure, neural patterns, memories, language, images, rewards, errors, probabilities, and environmental cues. A bacterium following a chemical gradient is using information. A raven solving a puzzle is using information. A child learning which adult is safe is using information. A language model predicting the next token is using information.

Action also needs to be broader than physical movement. Movement is action, but so is prediction, modeling, classification, speech, planning, selection, generation, tool use, and internal simulation. If a system predicts, chooses, outputs, or updates in response to information, it is acting in the sense that matters here.

Fit is the evolutionary bridge. In biology, fit may mean survival, reproduction, energy efficiency, predator avoidance, social success, attachment, play, or exploration. In machines, fit may mean loss reduction, reward maximization, benchmark performance, user approval, task success, market usefulness, or alignment with a feedback signal. Fit is not always morally good. It means effective under a pressure.

This definition does not require consciousness. It does not require human likeness. It does not require biological life, IQ, selfhood, or emotion. Those may be important forms, companions, or amplifiers of intelligence, but they should not be treated as the gatekeepers for every kind. Intelligence is adaptive fit-making. It is not a crown awarded to humans. It is a name for systems that use information to become more effective under pressure.

Once we define intelligence this way, it becomes harder to treat it as one thing.

## Intelligence Is Not One Thing

A baby is not unintelligent because it cannot do algebra. A baby is a developing intelligence with enormous learning capacity. A raven may not write essays, but it can use tools, remember locations, and solve spatial problems. A dog reads human social cues that many machines still cannot. A chimpanzee can solve physical puzzles and learn from others. An octopus explores the world with a body and nervous system very unlike ours. A nematode does not think in the human sense, but it can act adaptively through simple sensory loops.

The point is not to rank these systems on one ladder. The point is that intelligence is always tied to a system's world. It depends on bodies, sensors, histories, pressures, memories, and available actions. Asking whether an octopus is intelligent like us is less useful than asking what kind of problems an octopus has been shaped to solve.

The same principle should apply to machines. Asking whether AI is intelligent as if AI were one thing is like asking whether animals are intelligent. Which animal? In what environment? With what body? Under what pressure? Doing what? A raven, a dog, a nematode, and a human are all parts of the same broad conversation, but they do not possess the same intelligence in different quantities. They express different forms of adaptive fit-making.

If intelligences differ, the next question is how any system becomes smart in the first place.

## How Organisms Become Smart

Organisms do not begin as blank receivers of data. Before an animal senses anything, evolution has already shaped its body, senses, reflexes, drives, and nervous system. Inheritance comes first. The eye, the wing, the limb, the gut, the reflex, the fear response, the appetite, and the capacity to learn are all products of prior selection.

So the simple ladder of senses, then learning, then models, then language is useful but incomplete. Sensing and action arise together. Organisms do not passively collect data from the world. They move, eat, avoid, reach, explore, test, and fail. A creature senses in order to act, and acts in order to generate new sensory information.

Feedback gives the process direction. Hunger, pain, pleasure, fear, curiosity, attachment, social approval, and failure tell organisms what matters. Memory lets useful changes persist. Learning tunes behavior within a lifetime. Modeling allows organisms to anticipate patterns. Simulation allows richer organisms to test possibilities internally before acting. Social learning allows one organism to inherit knowledge from another. Language and tools make those inheritances cumulative.

Daniel Dennett offers a useful ladder for these layers: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures.

## Dennett's Creature Ladder

Darwinian creatures are shaped by selection across generations. Their competence is not learned by the individual so much as built into the organism through evolutionary history. A body plan, nervous system, instinct, reflex, or perceptual bias can embody intelligence because prior selection has fitted it to a world.

Skinnerian creatures learn within a lifetime. They try behaviors, receive consequences, and become more likely to repeat what works. Reward and punishment shape action. This lets organisms adapt faster than genetic evolution alone.

Popperian creatures can test possibilities internally before risking them externally. They can model, predict, imagine, or simulate. The useful phrase here is that hypotheses can die in our stead. Instead of learning only by costly failure, a creature can run a possible action through an internal model before acting in the world.

Gregorian creatures inherit mind-tools from culture. Language, maps, diagrams, numbers, writing, stories, institutions, scientific methods, and concepts allow individuals to use cognitive tools they did not invent. Human intelligence is deeply Gregorian because it is built from inherited cultural machinery.

Each layer changes the kind of intelligence possible. Evolution builds the organism. Reinforcement tunes behavior. Simulation lets possible failures happen internally. Culture and language let minds inherit tools from other minds.

The layers do not replace one another. Human beings are still Darwinian, Skinnerian, and Popperian while also being Gregorian. We are evolved bodies. We learn through reward and correction. We simulate futures before acting. We inherit language, maps, mathematics, tools, and institutions. Our intelligence is not sealed inside the skull. It is scaffolded by a world of shared artifacts.

Language matters because it does not merely express thought. It changes what thought can do.

## Language As A Mind-Tool

Words make categories portable. Writing makes memory external. Diagrams make relations inspectable. Equations make abstractions manipulable. Books let one mind work with the products of many others. A shopping list is not merely a record of memory; it changes what the person has to remember. A map is not merely a picture of terrain; it permits route-planning that unaided memory could not easily perform. Algebra is not merely a notation for thought; it permits operations that ordinary intuition cannot manage.

This is the force of Dennett's idea of mind-tools. Human beings do not just think and then use language to report the results. We use language, writing, symbols, and tools to think. Andy Clark and David Chalmers push this further with the extended mind: cognition can extend into the environment when external resources are reliably integrated into a thinking process. Notebooks, diagrams, calculators, code libraries, scientific papers, legal systems, and databases can become parts of practical cognition.

Human intelligence, then, is not just brain tissue firing in isolation. It is brain, body, language, tools, culture, and environment working together. We are already scaffolded intelligences.

This matters for machines because large language models are not trained on raw nature. They are trained on the residue of human mind-tools: explanations, procedures, arguments, stories, code, documentation, scientific records, jokes, laws, recipes, and abstractions. To call this "just text" is to misunderstand what text is. Text is one of humanity's main vehicles for externalized thought.

Still, language is not the same as understanding.

## Understanding Is More Than Decoding

Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson are useful here because they challenge the idea that communication is merely encoding and decoding. Human verbal understanding is inferential. We infer what someone means from context, intention, relevance, shared background, implication, tone, and social situation. The words matter, but the words do not do all the work.

That caution matters for LLMs. These systems can produce fluent, relevant, often useful text because they inherit vast patterns of human language. But fluency alone is not the whole of understanding. A system can reproduce patterns of explanation without possessing the same world-contact, stakes, memory, or social accountability that human communication depends on.

The point is not that machines only decode while humans understand. That is too simple. Understanding itself has layers. There is linguistic competence, pragmatic inference, embodied grounding, social accountability, practical consequence, and internal simulation. Machines can be strong in some layers and weak in others.

That unevenness is central to machine intelligence.

## How Machines Become Smart

Silicon is not inherently intelligent. But carbon is not inherently intelligent either. Intelligence appears when a substrate is organized by processes that create adaptive fit. For machines, those processes include architecture, data, training, prediction, error correction, reward, selection, simulation, memory-like weight structure, and deployment feedback.

The biological elements have machine corollaries. Inheritance becomes architecture, initialization, pretrained weights, datasets, prior model generations, and engineering assumptions. Sensory input becomes text, images, audio, video, game states, prompts, robot sensors, API results, and benchmark tasks. Feedback becomes loss functions, reward models, human ratings, failures, benchmark scores, and user corrections. Memory becomes weights, context windows, retrieval systems, fine-tuning, logs, and stored representations. Action becomes prediction, generation, classification, tool calls, code execution, game moves, robotic movement, and planning.

Machine intelligence is not born. It is trained, selected, corrected, and deployed.

## Machine Darwinism: Selection In The Lab

The Darwinian analogue appears in selection. Biological evolution is wild, open-ended, embodied, and multi-pressure. It selects through survival, reproduction, predators, climate, disease, hunger, social life, bodily constraints, accident, play, and death. Machine selection is narrower, faster, more intentional, and more instrumented.

Researchers choose architectures that perform better. Companies scale models that users adopt. Benchmarks reward some capabilities and neglect others. Training methods survive if they reduce loss, improve performance, or fit compute budgets. Products survive if they are useful, profitable, impressive, or easy to integrate. Entire approaches rise or fall based on performance, cost, reliability, and social demand.

This is not literal natural selection. A model does not reproduce sexually or die in a forest. But the broader pattern is selection among variants under pressure. The lab chooses the pressures, measures the outcomes, and keeps the lineages that work.

Biological intelligence is evolved in the wild. Machine intelligence is evolved in the lab. The lab does not make the process unreal. It makes it directed, compressed, and parameterized.

## Machine Skinnerism: Feedback, Reward, And Correction

The Skinnerian analogue appears in feedback, reward, and correction. Skinnerian creatures learn from consequences. They try something, receive feedback, and become more or less likely to do it again. Machine learning has many versions of this pattern: gradient descent, reinforcement learning, reward functions, RLHF, RLAIF, preference optimization, self-play, and user feedback.

AlphaGo and AlphaZero are clean examples. They operate in a constrained world where success is measurable: win the game. They explore possible moves, receive feedback, and improve through self-play and search. Their intelligence is not cultural in the way a language model's is. It is strategic, evaluative, and tightly coupled to a formal environment.

RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, is different. Human raters or preference models shape outputs toward what people find helpful, harmless, polite, safe, or useful. This does not give a model morality or lived social understanding. But it does act as a machine analogue of behavioral correction. Some outputs are rewarded. Others are discouraged. The model becomes better fitted to human preference as measured by the training process.

This also shows the danger of feedback. Feedback is not the same as truth. A system optimized for approval may become agreeable rather than accurate. A system optimized for benchmarks may learn benchmark-shaped competence. Feedback creates fit to a pressure, and the pressure matters.

## Machine Popperism: Simulation And World Models

The Popperian analogue appears in simulation and world models. Popperian intelligence lets hypotheses die before the organism does. In biological terms, this means prediction, imagination, planning, route simulation, social rehearsal, counterfactual thinking, and internal trial. A system does not need to learn only by external failure if it can test possibilities internally.

Machine versions include search, planning systems, model-based reinforcement learning, world models, tree search, simulators, chain-of-thought-like reasoning traces, and JEPA-style predictive latent models. AlphaGo's search is one example: it evaluates possible futures before choosing a move. JEPA and related approaches are interesting because they aim to learn abstract predictive structure rather than only mapping input to output.

LLMs complicate this layer. They may have a linguistic or conceptual model of the world because they absorb patterns from human descriptions. They know, in some functional textual sense, that glass breaks, promises create obligations, jokes can offend, Paris is in France, and causes precede effects. But that is not the same as an embodied, action-grounded world model built through direct survival in the world.

LLMs do not model the world the way animals do. They model the human record of world-modeling. That is not an insult. It is a precise description of their strength and limitation.

Their greatest strength is Gregorian.

## Machine Gregorianism: The Fossil Record Of Human Cognition

Large language models are trained on the accumulated symbolic output of human civilization: language, code, science, stories, essays, documentation, arguments, metaphors, laws, instructions, jokes, comments, tutorials, and explanations. They are not trained on the world directly. They are trained on the world as processed by humans.

They inherit our categories, abstractions, procedures, analogies, biases, myths, arguments, discoveries, and confusions. They are trained on the fossil record of human cognition.

This is why "just text" is too dismissive. Text contains recipes, laws, proofs, philosophies, engineering manuals, novels, bug reports, mathematical notation, historical records, social scripts, and scientific theories. Text is compressed human experience and cultural memory. When machines train on text, they train on the externalized products of human thought.

Machine Gregorian intelligence is derivative in one sense. It rests on human cultural capital. But human Gregorian intelligence is also derivative. Every educated human inherits tools, concepts, words, maps, metaphors, norms, and institutions they did not invent. The difference is that humans inherit them as embodied organisms living in the world. LLMs inherit them as machine systems trained on symbolic residue.

This is the magic of the system: we built mind-tools for one another, stored them in shareable media, then trained machines on those stores. Machine training now runs over the archive of human culture.

But LLMs are not all of machine intelligence.

## Not All AI Is The Same

Asking whether AI is intelligent is like asking whether animals are intelligent. The category is too broad. Different systems exhibit different facets because they are built with different architectures, trained in different environments, optimized for different goals, and given different forms of action.

LLMs showcase language, cultural inheritance, synthesis, explanation, and tool-mediated work. AlphaGo and AlphaZero showcase reinforcement, self-play, search, and strategic discovery. RLHF systems showcase human preference shaping and social correction. JEPA and world-model research showcase predictive abstraction and internal simulation. Robotics showcases perception, action, embodiment, friction, failure, and physical feedback. Tool-using agents showcase action in symbolic environments: calling APIs, writing code, searching databases, and modifying files.

No single system mirrors the whole organismic stack. LLMs are profoundly Gregorian, partially Skinnerian through post-training, and linguistically Popperian in some situations. AlphaGo is strongly Skinnerian and Popperian but not Gregorian in the same way. Robots may be more embodied but less culturally fluent. Machine intelligence is plural because organismic intelligence is plural.

Plural intelligence also means plural failure.

## Ralph Wiggum Mode

One vivid failure mode is what we might call Ralph Wiggum mode. This is when a system produces fluent language that has the shape of understanding but collapses into absurdity, literalness, misplaced confidence, or context-misalignment. It sounds as if the system inherited the sentence pattern without inheriting enough of the world-structure behind it.

This should not be a cheap joke or a dismissal. It diagnoses a real weakness of Gregorian-heavy machine intelligence. A model can inherit the speech patterns of intelligence without inheriting the full organismic conditions that made those patterns meaningful: bodies, needs, mortality, direct perception, developmental history, social accountability, and practical stakes.

Ralph Wiggum mode is what happens when Gregorian intelligence outruns Popperian and embodied intelligence. The model has the cultural artifact, but not enough grounded simulation or consequence to keep it attached to reality.

This brings us back to Sperber and Wilson. Verbal understanding is not just producing plausible words. It requires context, intention, relevance, and practical fit. Machine systems can approximate some of these through training, but the approximation is uneven.

That unevenness is not a reason to deny machine intelligence. It is a reason to describe it accurately.

## What Machine Intelligence Has And Lacks

Machine intelligence can have real adaptive capacities: prediction, pattern recognition, abstraction, linguistic fluency, cross-domain synthesis, tool use, planning-like behavior, code generation, strategic search, and responsiveness to feedback. Some systems can act in digital environments, solve formal problems, cooperate with humans, and improve through training or deployment loops.

But machine systems generally lack organismic need, metabolism, mortality, childhood, biological embodiment, intrinsic survival pressure, direct lived experience, durable personal identity, and full sensorimotor grounding. Their goals are usually imposed, not lived. Their feedback is engineered, not existential. Their worlds are datasets, games, simulators, prompts, benchmarks, APIs, and product environments.

An animal learns because the world can hurt, feed, threaten, reward, or kill it. A model learns because a training process adjusts parameters in response to error, reward, or preference. Both are feedback, but not the same kind of feedback.

So machine intelligence should not be treated as basically mind, and it should not be dismissed as nothing but statistics. Its capacities are real but production-specific. It bears the marks of the processes that made it.

This is what I mean by intelligence grown under glass.

## Intelligence Grown Under Glass

Machine intelligence is lab-grown intelligence. The phrase is both technical and conceptual. Technically, these systems are grown through controlled training runs, selected objectives, finite parameters, instrumentation, compute budgets, evaluation suites, reward models, and deployment feedback. Conceptually, the phrase explains both their power and their distortion.

Biological intelligence is evolved in the wild. It is shaped by messy, overlapping, open-ended pressures. Machine intelligence is evolved in the lab. It is shaped by targeted pressures chosen by researchers, companies, benchmarks, users, and markets. The process is artificial, but artificial does not mean unreal.

A lab-grown crystal is still a crystal. Cultured tissue is still tissue. A domesticated animal is still an evolved animal. But production history matters. Lab-grown intelligence carries the marks of the laboratory: brittleness outside distribution, benchmark-shaped competence, dependence on human data, optimization for approval, lack of embodied stakes, sudden gaps, and surprising strengths.

The artificiality of machine intelligence tells us what kind of intelligence it is. It is directed, compressed, cultural, engineered, and uneven. It is not wild intelligence, but it is not fake intelligence.

## The Non-Magical Explanation Is Astonishing

That is why the god-versus-autocomplete binary fails. Machine intelligence is not a god, not a person, not a conscious animal, and not merely autocomplete. It is a lab-grown form of adaptive competence produced by machine processes acting on human cultural inheritance.

Return to the working definition: intelligence is adaptive fit-making. Machine systems exhibit intelligence when they use information to select actions that fit tasks, environments, or goals. Their actions may be predictions, generated sentences, game moves, tool calls, plans, classifications, or code. Their fit may be to a loss function, reward signal, benchmark, user request, or deployment environment.

The non-magical explanation is astonishing enough. We have built systems that run training, feedback, selection, simulation, and optimization over the externalized products of human thought. They do not need to be human to matter. They do not need to be conscious to exhibit intelligence. They need to be understood as the kind of thing they are: machine intelligence, grown under glass.

The question is no longer whether machines think exactly as humans do. It is whether intelligence can be produced wherever the right adaptive processes are organized. Machine intelligence suggests that the answer is yes, but that the resulting intelligence always bears the shape of its substrate, environment, pressures, and inheritance.
