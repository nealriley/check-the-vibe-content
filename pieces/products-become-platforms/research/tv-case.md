# TV modification as a starting case

Research note, 25 October 2026. This records what the starting sources say and what remains an editorial hypothesis. The writer supplied the text of The Verge article; the article URL did not load in the browser tool during this pass. Its full text is not reproduced here.

## Sources

- John Higgins, [“People are vibe coding their way to better TVs,” *The Verge*](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1016116/vibe-coding-tv-os), 23 October 2026. The writer supplied a pasted copy for this research pass.
- [cleanyourtv project repository](https://github.com/Gathin23/cleanyourtv/), read 25 October 2026. Its README is a project's account of what its tools do, not an independent test of every supported device or privacy claim.

## What the sources establish

- The Verge reports a wave of projects including cleanyourtv, tv-detox, and dumbtv. Users aim to remove sponsored content and clutter, change the home screen, and limit some tracking. The report treats frustration with the default TV experience as a central motive.
- The practical route is mostly Android TV or Google TV: developer access, Android Debug Bridge, package controls, and alternative launchers. The Verge notes that these capabilities predate AI-assisted coding. AI can lower the effort of using and adapting them; it is not the source of the underlying access.
- The cleanyourtv README describes backing up settings and package lists, disabling selected packages, installing a launcher, and offering a restore path. It also documents device-specific limits. Those are the project's claims and instructions, not a guarantee for every TV.
- The Verge distinguishes these Android-based cases from Samsung Tizen, LG webOS, Roku, and other systems with different access constraints. It also cautions that blocking some destinations cannot establish that a connected TV sends no data, and that modifying a device can go wrong.

## What the essay might argue

The case suggests a shift from *using a product as supplied* to *treating it as material for a personally chosen experience*. If accessible software surfaces and reusable modifications let owners build on an object, the object can act like a platform for them. A coding agent broadens who can try this. The case does **not** yet show that every consumer product is becoming open, that most owners will modify their devices, or that the maker has ceded lasting control.

The most interesting conflict may be over authorship of the experience. A manufacturer may want the home screen to promote content or support advertising; an owner may want a quiet route to the apps and inputs they chose. The ability to rewrite the interface makes that conflict concrete.

## Follow-up questions

- Which exact modifications were made by an agent, by an existing script, and by the user? How much skill did each route require?
- Can the change survive an OS update, and can the owner reliably undo it?
- Are project participants building new interfaces, sharing reusable modifications, or mainly applying the same existing debloat recipe?
- Which other product category has both accessible modification points and a clear user reason to remake the experience?
- How do manufacturers, maintainers, and ordinary owners describe the benefits and costs of this kind of control?
