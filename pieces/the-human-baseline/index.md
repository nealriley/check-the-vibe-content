# The Human Baseline

## The idea

An essay about what students and people early in their careers may need to make for themselves in an AI-saturated working world. If a fluent first draft is always available, where do a person's taste, judgement, technical understanding, and distinctive voice come from? One answer worth exploring is a personal cache of work they made and thought through themselves: code, prose, sketches, decisions, revisions, and accounts of what they learned. Later, they can draw on that material when using AI, rather than asking a general model to supply their starting point every time.

Radiocarbon dating provides the opening analogy: atmospheric nuclear tests changed the reference signal that scientists had to interpret. The analogy needs care. The tests did **not** make radiocarbon dating impossible; their carbon-14 spike can itself date recent material. The useful parallel may be the need for a known baseline and careful calibration when the surrounding material changes.

This is a working argument, not a claim that AI-assisted work lacks human authorship or that juniors should avoid AI. The central question is how to use assistance while still developing a self-authored body of experience and material to draw on.

## Research

- [Concept research and source notes](research/concept-notes.md) covers radiocarbon science, AI output diversity and model training, skill formation, and personal context for AI systems. It marks the limits of each analogy and finding.

The follow-up research sharpened one distinction: AI can expand what a junior can produce today without necessarily building the judgement they can use unaided later. It also found counterexamples to a blanket claim that AI makes everyone’s output more alike. The personal cache remains a proposed way to develop and supply independent context, not a proven solution.

## Questions to develop

- What would count as a useful personal cache: polished work, rough thinking, rejected options, or all three?
- Which tasks benefit from an unaided first attempt, and which are better learned with an AI tutor or collaborator?
- How can someone record authorship and degrees of assistance without turning their archive into a purity test?
- Can a personal archive give a model more relevant context while also giving its owner a clearer sense of their own style?
- What concrete example could show the difference between an AI-generated default and a person's own decision?
- How should we compare assisted output, immediate understanding, and skill retained after the tool is removed?
