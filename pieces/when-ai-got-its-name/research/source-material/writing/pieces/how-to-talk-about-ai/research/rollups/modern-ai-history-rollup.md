## Approved language / draftable explanation

The term “artificial intelligence” entered the field’s formal agenda in the 1955 proposal for a summer research project at Dartmouth in 1956. The workshop is often remembered as a founding moment because it gave a scattered set of ambitions—machines using language, forming abstractions, solving human-reserved problems, improving themselves—a name and a research program. But Dartmouth did not create the dream of machine intelligence from nothing; it named and organized work already shaped by computation, cybernetics, logic, games, and earlier mechanical dreams.

After Dartmouth, one influential path tried to make intelligence explicit in symbols, rules, search procedures, and knowledge representations. Expert systems such as DENDRAL and MYCIN show the promise and limit of that approach: narrow-domain knowledge could be useful, but hand-built rules and brittle expectations did not add up to general intelligence. The later story should not be told as one clean boom and bust. AI winter is a useful label for cooled funding and confidence, especially after the 1980s expert-system boom, but the timing and severity varied by institution, country, metric, and subfield.

The modern line then turns toward systems trained from data: machine learning, neural networks, backpropagation, ImageNet/AlexNet, GPUs, and eventually larger-scale language models. The Transformer paper in 2017 is the key architecture milestone to name carefully. ChatGPT's November 30, 2022 public launch can close the chapter as the moment many readers met generative AI through a conversational product, while the surrounding wave and risks should be sourced beyond OpenAI's announcement.
